# Voice audition passages

All passages below are invented test material, not Diaspora canon. Copy only the text beneath each sample heading. Use identical text for every voice. Short samples each fit the unsigned-in Azure gallery's 500-character field.

## Sample 1 — narrative and dialogue

The station fell silent as the last ship disappeared beyond the horizon. For a moment, Mara waited. Was the signal a warning, or an invitation? "Listen again," she said. "There is something between the words." The recording began softly, then stopped. Nobody moved. Outside, the first lights of morning reached the empty landing field.

## Sample 2 — explanation and listening comfort

A useful archive preserves more than conclusions. It records the observations, the uncertainties, and the reasons a conclusion might change. Two accounts can disagree without either being dishonest. One witness may have seen the beginning; another, the aftermath. Read both carefully, separate evidence from interpretation, and leave room for a better explanation.

## Sample 3 — numbers, names, and pronunciation

At 7:45 a.m., Dr. Imani opened File IV. The sensor reported 0.8 g, a temperature of -12 degrees Celsius, and a distance of 1,250 km. The repair would cost $42.50. AXIOM listed three destinations: Kepler, Tau Ceti, and Epsilon Eridani. "Is that fifteen or fifty?" Mara asked. "Fifteen," he replied. "Check the decimal point before you send the report."

## Pronunciation notes for the listener

In Sample 3, File IV means File Four; g means the spoken letter gee as a measure of acceleration; km means kilometers; AXIOM is the word axiom, not five separate letters. Record how each untreated voice handles these. Do not assume invented personal names have a single mandatory pronunciation. For a corrected round, agree on their pronunciations and apply equivalent replacements to every finalist.

## Long passage — sustained reading and following

The morning report arrived before the lights in the reading room had reached their full brightness. Mara set her cup beside the console and opened the first page. The title was familiar, but the date was wrong. Someone had filed a recent observation under an event that had happened twelve years earlier. She checked the record again. The date remained unchanged, and the quiet room seemed suddenly less ordinary than it had a moment before.

There were several possible explanations. A clerk might have copied an old heading. A damaged clock might have supplied the wrong time. The report itself might be a reconstruction, written long after the original event. None of these possibilities required a remarkable discovery. Before choosing the most interesting explanation, Mara decided to eliminate the simple ones. She made a note beside the title and continued reading, keeping the uncertainty visible rather than hiding it behind a confident label.

The first witness described a light above the western ridge. It appeared shortly after sunset and remained visible for nearly a minute. The second witness described a sound from the same direction, but placed it earlier in the evening. A third account mentioned neither light nor sound. Instead, it recorded that every instrument in the small weather station had restarted at once. Each observation was useful. None, taken alone, could explain what had happened.

"Start with the clocks," Imani said from the doorway. Mara looked up. He was carrying a folder that appeared older than either of them. Inside were handwritten maintenance records, receipts for replacement batteries, and a diagram of the station's electrical system. The instrument clocks had shared a power supply. The wall clock had not. That difference did not solve the mystery, but it gave them a practical place to begin. They spread the records across the table and checked each entry against the report.

As the morning passed, the story became less dramatic and more precise. One instrument had stopped recording long before the others. Another had been adjusted by a technician who had failed to update the maintenance sheet. The witness who remembered the light had estimated the time from a radio program. The witness who remembered the sound had used the wall clock. Their accounts still differed, but the disagreement now had a shape. It could be investigated rather than merely repeated.

This is the long paragraph for the screen-following check. A listener should be able to keep reading without reaching for the screen every few seconds. The highlighted sentence should move forward when the voice reaches it, even though the paragraph has not ended. If the listener pauses, the highlight should wait. If the listener increases the reading speed, the text should continue to follow the actual sound. Scrolling back to an earlier sentence should suspend automatic following, leaving the listener free to review what came before. Returning to the spoken passage should take one clear action. Neither a note nor a search result should move the reading position unexpectedly. The controls should remain visible, and the current sentence should never be hidden underneath them. These behaviors matter most when the text is longer than the screen, because a paragraph can remain active long after its first sentence has disappeared from view.

By noon, Mara had written a new summary. It was shorter than the original and contained fewer claims. That did not make it less useful. The revised version distinguished the times that had been measured from those that had been remembered. It explained which instruments shared a power source and which observations could be checked independently. At the end, she added a single unresolved question. The light above the ridge still had no confirmed explanation.

Imani read the summary twice. "You have made the uncertainty much clearer," he said. Mara smiled. She had expected him to ask whether they had discovered anything important. Instead, he pointed to the maintenance diagram and suggested one more comparison. A delivery vehicle had passed the station that evening. Its onboard clock might provide an independent reference. They would need permission to inspect the record, and the record might no longer exist, but the next step was at least specific.

The archive saved both versions of the report. Readers could see the original observations, the later corrections, and the reasons for each change. Nothing had been silently erased. When Mara closed the file, the room was bright with afternoon light. The mystery was smaller now, but it was also more honest. She returned her cup to the tray, marked the question for follow-up, and moved to the next document. This is the final sentence of the audition passage.
